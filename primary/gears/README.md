# How Gears Transfer Motion

A Primary Science lesson: identify gears as simple machines and predict the opposite rotation directions of two externally meshing gears. Open `index.html` directly, offline. Curriculum note supplied by the user: gears are one of the simple machines; no textbook page range was supplied.

Figure concept: a hand crank drives one visible spur gear and its teeth drive a second wheel on a parallel axle. Colored markers make the opposite rotations easy to follow. Changing tooth counts changes wheel size and center spacing while preserving mesh.

Parts plan: a bench on four rubber feet; inset base plate; two axle-carriage bases and bearing blocks; shafts, collars and hub caps; two thick gear wheels with 18, 24 or 36 teeth; three inset web panels and three mounting fasteners per wheel; two index marks; a crank arm, pin and grip; two captive carriage rails with end stops and screws; two engraved label plates. More than 40 purposeful components including teeth; shaded solids and back-to-front placement give depth. The driver is orange; the driven gear is teal.

## Controls and physics

Choose 18, 24 or 36 teeth for each gear. Turn ½ advances the driver by half a turn in the selected direction; Reverse changes the next turn direction. Drag the crank around its axle or use the rotation slider (−720° to +720°). Arrow keys step by 5°, Shift by 45°, Home/End select the limits. Auto explore turns the pair continuously within these bounds and reverses at the ends. Pause stops the tour; motion settles to the current target. Reset restores 18 driving teeth, 36 driven teeth, zero turns and clockwise as the next direction. Changing a gear resets the turn counters while remeshing the pair. Reduced motion uses immediate rotation and a single half-turn on Auto explore. Hidden/offscreen animations pause; explicit controls still update immediately if scrolled offscreen.

Fullscreen keeps the same Turn ½ and Reverse buttons, live output and an anchored crank indicator. The same buttons return to the normal control bar on exit. Escape, focus restoration, native fullscreen and fallback use the shared behavior.

For external spur gears, N₁θ₁ + N₂θ₂ = 0, so θ₂ = −θ₁N₁/N₂. Here θ is signed turns from reset, not absolute marker orientation; 1 turn = 360°. Positive driver rotation is clockwise viewed from above; negative is counterclockwise. Instantaneous direction labels follow motion, or the selected next direction at rest. Equal tooth counts give equal speeds; fewer driven teeth give a higher driven speed. The graph is the same relation with a live point. It is not a time graph. No torque, efficiency, inertia, backlash, friction, tooth bending or motor behavior is simulated.

Both wheels use module 2 mm, 20° pressure-angle involute flanks, pitch radius r = mN/2, addendum radius r + m, and root radius r − 1.25m. Centers are separated by the sum of pitch radii. The minimum 18-tooth choice avoids the usual small-tooth-count undercut problem for this profile. The driven gear starts half a tooth out of phase at the contact line, and its phase is derived from the same turn ratio as the readout. Involutes are sampled with 16 segments per flank; tooth tips and root arcs are polygonal approximations. The root-to-base connection is radial rather than a manufactured trochoidal fillet. The thick gear bodies are extrusions; shafts and hub caps cover the central bore in the illustration. Decorative web recesses are shallow top-face inlays. Crank bearings and axle carriages are illustrative, with center spacing recalculated for each pair. The near-top-down isometric camera keeps teeth readable and the pair close up; directions are viewed along the axles.
